About the Area Forecast Discussion

What Is an AFD?

An Area Forecast Discussion (AFD) is a text product issued several times daily by each of the 122 National Weather Service Weather Forecast Offices (WFOs) across the United States. Unlike the machine-friendly forecasts that populate weather apps, the AFD is a forecaster writing in their own voice — explaining why the weather is doing what it's doing and what they expect to happen next.

Each AFD typically covers the synoptic-scale (large-scale) weather pattern, the short-term forecast (next day or two), the extended outlook, and any hazardous weather considerations. Some offices also include marine, aviation, fire weather, and hydrology sections. Some mountain regions even get avalanche forecasts!

What Does "Synoptic" Mean?

The word synoptic comes from the Greek synoptikos, meaning "seen together" — a view of the whole. In meteorology, the synoptic scale refers to weather systems large enough to be seen on a continental map: fronts, pressure systems, and jet stream patterns that span hundreds to thousands of miles.

This is where the AFD begins. Forecasters start with the synoptic-scale view — the big atmospheric picture — and then work their way down to what it means for your specific area. A low-pressure system tracking across the Pacific (synoptic) might bring rain to the Bay Area (local). An upper-level ridge building over the West (synoptic) might mean a heat wave in your backyard (local).

How to Read an AFD

AFDs are written in a shorthand that can seem intimidating at first. Forecasters often use abbreviations like "sfc" (surface), "aloft" (upper atmosphere), and reference numerical weather models by name — GFS, ECMWF (the "Euro"), NAM, HRRR. Don't worry if you don't know what these mean yet; that's exactly why we built the glossary.

The most important thing to know: the AFD tells you the story of the weather. Start with the Synopsis for the big picture, then read the Short Term for what's coming soon. The Long Term gives you the week-ahead outlook. Sections like Aviation and Marine serve specialized audiences hitting the water or the skies.

The Forecasters

Every AFD is written and signed by a human meteorologist working at a local Weather Forecast Office. These are highly trained professionals who blend numerical model guidance with their deep understanding of local geography, microclimates, and weather patterns.

The AFD I read daily for my home in the San Francisco Bay Area is produced by the WFO in Monterey, California (office code MTR). This office covers a region with huge geographic and climatic diversity — from coastal fog to inland valleys, mountain snow to urban microclimates. Their forecasters are experts in the interplay between the Pacific Ocean, the Coast Ranges, and the Central Valley.

Your Weather Forecast Office

There are 122 Weather Forecast Offices scattered across the United States, Puerto Rico, and Guam. Each one is staffed around the clock, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, by human meteorologists. Many WFOs launch weather balloons (radiosondes) twice daily at 00Z and 12Z — roughly 4 PM and 4 AM Pacific Standard Time. These instruments ascend through the atmosphere, measuring temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind from the surface up to around 100,000 feet. That data feeds directly into the numerical weather models that forecasters use to write the AFD.

WFOs also rely on the NEXRAD (WSR-88D) Doppler radar network for a real-time view of precipitation, wind patterns, and severe weather. When you see a radar loop on TV or a weather app, it often comes from one of these 160 radars scattered across the country.

Beyond the AFD, your WFO issues local warnings, watches, and advisories; produces zone forecasts; supports emergency management; and serves as the authoritative local voice of the National Weather Service.

Why This Matters

In an age of algorithmic weather apps and AI-generated forecasts, the AFD is a reminder that weather forecasting is fundamentally a human endeavor. Numerical models provide the raw ingredients, but it takes a skilled forecaster to weigh conflicting model guidance, account for local effects, and communicate uncertainty.

All raw data on this site comes directly from the National Weather Service and is free, public, and open. We don't modify the forecast text itself. To give you a better sense of your specific location's forecast in the context of the AFD, the AI summary tool combines the point forecast for your location with the AFD.

Why I Built This

I've been a weather nerd for as long as I can remember. In elementary school, I did three consecutive science fair projects on weather, built backyard weather stations, and spent way more time than normal for a child my age watching the Weather Channel local forecasts. My education and career has always been centered on science, technology, and policy, and briefly intersected with the weather after grad school when I was an analyst at NOAA working to secure over a $1 billlion in federal resources for the National Weather Service. While I don't work on the weather now, I still track local and ski forecasts closely (shout out to Open Snow) and have never lost the taste for Weather Channel-inspired Smooth Jazz Funk.

In recent years, the AFD has become one of the first things I check in the morning, and I always enjoy booting it up on travels to see how different forecasters describe the atmospheric trends where they are. Synoptic Skies exists to make this incredible public resource accessible to anyone who's curious about what's happening in the sky above them.
